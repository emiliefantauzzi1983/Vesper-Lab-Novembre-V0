Storytelling in luxury: the story is in the object

At Louis Vuitton, before you learn to sell a watch, you learn how it is made. I went through the house's craftsmanship modules across leather goods, fine jewellery, watches and fragrance: the materials, the gestures, the hours behind each piece. It felt like a detour from selling. It was the opposite. It was the selling.

Because here is what the word storytelling hides: in most companies it means campaign copy. A film, a tagline, a mood. In the great houses it means something else. It means every person on the floor can give an object its history, in one minute, to the client standing in front of it.

Three layers of story

Watch a trained seller present a piece and you hear three layers. The material layer: what this is made of, where it comes from, whose hands worked on it. The house layer: why this object exists, what tradition or rupture it carries, where it sits in the maison's history. And the client layer, the one that matters most: the story this person will tell when someone asks about it at dinner.

That third layer is the test. A client does not buy your heritage. A client buys what your heritage lets them say about themselves. The seller's craft is to connect the object's story to the client's story, without forcing either.

Storytelling is not a script

The mistake I see brands make when they discover this: they write stories for their sellers and ask them to recite. It fails within a week. A recited story sounds recited, and in luxury a false note costs more than silence.

What works is what the houses do. Give your teams true material: the workshop visit, the maker's name, the reason this stitch and not another, the failure that led to the design. Train the material until it is owned, then let each seller find their own words. A story you own comes out different every time and sincere every time.

You do not need a century of heritage

The objection I hear in Copenhagen: we are twelve years old, we have no heritage to tell. Wrong problem. You have a founder who started somewhere, a material you chose over a cheaper one, a product that failed twice before it worked, a workshop or a factory with people in it. That is story material. What is missing is not history. What is missing is the work of collecting it, writing it and training it until your newest hire can give any product its minute of story.

The brands that do this work stop competing on the shelf. The product next to yours has a price. Yours has a price and a story. Over time, that difference is the margin.
