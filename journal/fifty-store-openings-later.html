Fifty store openings later: what decides opening day

I have delivered more than fifty store openings, refurbishments and concept rollouts, from China to Dubai to Moscow. Here is the pattern behind every one that went wrong: the failure never happened on opening day. It happened six weeks before, in a briefing nobody wrote and a delivery nobody chased.

The date does not move. Everything else does.

An opening is a strange project. The deadline is public, printed on invitations, sometimes announced in the press. Meanwhile every input is unstable: the site delivers late, the furniture ships from another continent, the local team is hired three weeks before, and the architect's plans meet a wall that is not where the drawing says it is.

Teams that treat an opening like a normal project get eaten by this asymmetry. The method that survives it has three parts.

Retroplan from the ribbon backwards

You do not plan an opening forwards from today. You plan it backwards from the ribbon: which day the team rehearses, which day the product hits the floor, which day the installation must be done, which day the last container must clear customs. Each date has an owner. When a date slips, the plan shows you the same hour what it costs downstream, and you decide with open eyes instead of discovering it on site.

Brief vendors like they cannot read your mind

Because they cannot. The expensive mistakes I have seen, wrong finishes, wrong dimensions, fixtures that arrive in the wrong sequence, trace back to a briefing that lived in someone's head or in a phone call. A vendor brief is written, visual and technical: reference images, exact specifications, tolerances and a named person who answers questions. Boring to produce. Cheaper than a reorder from China with three weeks to go.

Be on site for the final days

The last week of an opening is a hundred small decisions a day: this light hits the glass wrong, this wall needs the product two centimetres lower, this team member has never done a launch morning. None of these can be settled by email from headquarters. Someone with the authority to decide and the eye to see must stand in the store. Every opening I have seen go calm had that person. Every one that turned into a panic was missing them.

The paradox of a well-run opening is that opening day feels like nothing. The team rehearsed, the store is ready a day early, and the only thing left to do is welcome people. That calm is not luck. It is the plan, executed weeks earlier, doing its work.
