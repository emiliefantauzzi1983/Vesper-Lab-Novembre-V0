What the great houses know about welcoming a customer

My first job in retail was on the floor of the Louis Vuitton flagship in Paris, 1,800 square metres of it. Before I was allowed to serve a single customer, I was trained in the sales ceremony: the house's codified ritual for how a client is welcomed, listened to, presented with the product and seen out the door.

The word ceremony sounds grand. The content is not. It is a sequence of small, concrete behaviours: at what point you greet, what you say and what you never say, how you present a product with two hands, how you handle a hesitation, how you close without pressure, how you say goodbye so the person wants to come back. Nothing in it requires talent. All of it requires a decision.

Most brands leave this to chance

In the stores I visit today, the welcome depends on who is on shift. One seller has warmth and instinct, the next one hides behind the till. The brand has spent millions on the concept, the fit-out and the campaign, and then leaves the first thirty seconds of the customer's visit to individual personality.

That is a strange place to stop investing. The customer does not experience your brand book. The customer experiences a person, in a room, at a moment. If that moment is not designed, your brand is whatever that person improvises.

What a written ritual changes

When the ceremony is written, three things happen. Recruitment gets easier, because you hire for attitude and train the rest. Consistency stops depending on your best people being on shift. And your teams gain confidence, because the hardest moments of the job, the hesitation, the objection, the refusal, come with a known response instead of a private panic.

There is a misunderstanding worth clearing: a codified ritual is not a script. A script kills sincerity. A ritual is a structure inside which each seller keeps their own voice. The houses that do this well train the structure hard and then let the person breathe inside it.

What Nordic retail can take from this

The service culture in Copenhagen leans discreet: customers are left in peace, and staff wait to be needed. There is respect in that. There is also a missed opportunity, because discretion without design becomes absence.

The answer is not to import the Paris flagship playbook word for word. It is to write your own ceremony, at your price point, for your customer, in your culture, and then train it with the same seriousness the great houses do. The ritual will look different on Strøget than on the Champs-Élysées. The decision to have one is the same.
